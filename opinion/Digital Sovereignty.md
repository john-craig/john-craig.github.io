
## Cyber Gardens
Consider each person like a garden. Each device we interact with and each application we utilize can be likened to a plant growing in this garden. 
These plants, in turn, bear fruit: the posts were write, the images and videos of ourselves we upload. In other words, our content. But the plants 
also bear a much subtler fruit, because as we are using these applications, they collect far more information that what we expressly submit to 
them through their user interfaces.

An application tracks how long a user spends viewing it, tracks what the user clicks on within the application, and it tracks our eye movements,
so that it knows what we want to click on, but don't. It listens to our environment to track what we are doing and who we are with. And of course,
it tracks out location. This is the subtler fruit: metrics.

This fruit is very, very valuable. And yet, it is stolen from us. Like a tresspasser entering out garden and scything away a patch of strawberries,
the companies that build and run these applications gather metrics about us and keep them for themselves. There is a temptation to defend this action:
aren't the companies that create these applications more entitled to the metrics of their users than the user themselves? the companies are the ones
who have done all the work of creating not only the application itself, but also the mechanisms for the collection of the metrics.

As a response, ask yourself, does a person who has done all the work of building their own harvester combine and driving it across a farmer's land have
any claim whatsoever to the crops they collect?

Perhaps further, one would say that the creation of these metrics is a shared effort between application and user. But this is untrue. It is only by user
interaction, and nothing else, that any content or metrics-- any fruit-- is born on these applications.
